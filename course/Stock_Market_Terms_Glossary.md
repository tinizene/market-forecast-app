# Stock Market Terms Every Beginner Should Know

*An original glossary — organized by category, with a few extra terms thrown in that beginner lists often skip.*

---

## 1. Market Basics

**Stock (Share/Equity)** — A unit of ownership in a company. Owning a share entitles you to a proportional claim on the company's assets and earnings.

**Stock Market** — The collection of exchanges and marketplaces where shares of publicly held companies are bought and sold.

**Stock Exchange** — A regulated venue (like the NYSE or Nasdaq) where securities are listed and traded according to formal rules.

**Ticker Symbol** — The unique series of letters assigned to a company's stock for trading purposes (e.g., AAPL for Apple).

**Bull Market** — A sustained period of rising prices, generally reflecting investor optimism.

**Bear Market** — A sustained period of falling prices, typically defined as a drop of 20% or more from a recent high.

**IPO (Initial Public Offering)** — The first sale of a private company's shares to the public, converting it into a publicly traded company.

**Primary Market** — Where new securities are issued and sold directly by the company to investors (as in an IPO).

**Secondary Market** — Where existing securities are traded between investors after their initial issuance — this is what most people mean by "the stock market" day to day.

**Blue-Chip Stock** — Shares of large, well-established, financially sound companies with a long track record of stable performance.

**Penny Stock** — A stock that trades at a very low price (often under $5), typically issued by small or newer companies and associated with higher volatility and risk.

---

## 2. Buying, Selling, and Order Types

**Bid Price** — The highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay for a share.

**Ask Price (Offer)** — The lowest price a seller is currently willing to accept for a share.

**Spread** — The gap between the bid and ask price; a narrower spread generally signals a more liquid, actively traded stock.

**Market Order** — An order to buy or sell immediately at the best available current price.

**Limit Order** — An order to buy or sell only at a specified price or better.

**Stop Order (Stop-Loss Order)** — An order that triggers a market order once a stock hits a specified price, typically used to limit losses.

**Stop-Limit Order** — A hybrid order that triggers a limit order (rather than a market order) once a stock reaches a specified stop price.

**Day Order** — An order that expires automatically at the end of the trading day if not filled.

**Good-Til-Canceled (GTC) Order** — An order that remains active until it is filled or manually canceled, rather than expiring at day's end.

**Fill** — The completion of a buy or sell order.

**Round Lot** — A standard trading unit, typically 100 shares.

**Odd Lot** — An order for fewer than a standard round lot (e.g., 37 shares).

---

## 3. Ownership, Value, and Company Fundamentals

**Market Capitalization ("Market Cap")** — The total value of a company's outstanding shares, calculated as share price × number of shares outstanding.

**Outstanding Shares** — The total number of a company's shares currently held by all shareholders, including insiders and the public.

**Float** — The number of shares actually available for public trading, excluding closely held or restricted shares.

**Earnings Per Share (EPS)** — A company's net profit divided by its number of outstanding shares; a core measure of profitability per share.

**Price-to-Earnings Ratio (P/E Ratio)** — A stock's share price divided by its EPS, used to gauge whether a stock is expensive or cheap relative to its earnings.

**Price-to-Book Ratio (P/B Ratio)** — A stock's market price compared to its book value (net asset value) per share.

**Dividend** — A portion of a company's profits distributed to shareholders, usually paid quarterly in cash.

**Dividend Yield** — Annual dividend payments expressed as a percentage of the current share price.

**Dividend Reinvestment Plan (DRIP)** — A program that automatically uses dividend payouts to purchase additional shares rather than paying out cash.

**Book Value** — A company's total assets minus its total liabilities; a rough measure of what shareholders would theoretically receive if the company were liquidated.

**Revenue** — The total income a company generates from its business activities before expenses are deducted.

**Net Income (Profit)** — What remains of revenue after all expenses, taxes, and costs are subtracted.

**Market Sector** — A broad category of companies grouped by the type of business they engage in (e.g., technology, healthcare, energy).

**Industry** — A more specific classification within a sector (e.g., "semiconductors" within the technology sector).

---

## 4. Indices and Benchmarks

**Stock Index (Index)** — A statistical measure tracking the combined performance of a selected group of stocks, used as a benchmark for a market or sector.

**S&P 500** — An index tracking 500 of the largest publicly traded U.S. companies, widely used as a proxy for the overall U.S. stock market.

**Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA)** — A price-weighted index tracking 30 large, prominent U.S. companies.

**Nasdaq Composite** — An index tracking essentially all stocks listed on the Nasdaq exchange, heavily weighted toward technology companies.

**Benchmark** — A standard (often an index) against which the performance of an investment or portfolio is measured.

**Index Fund** — A fund designed to track the performance of a specific index rather than trying to outperform it.

---

## 5. Funds and Pooled Investing

**Mutual Fund** — A pooled investment vehicle that collects money from many investors to buy a diversified portfolio of securities, typically priced once per day.

**Exchange-Traded Fund (ETF)** — A fund holding a basket of securities that, unlike a mutual fund, trades on an exchange throughout the day like an individual stock.

**Net Asset Value (NAV)** — The per-share value of a fund's total assets minus its liabilities.

**Expense Ratio** — The annual fee a fund charges investors, expressed as a percentage of assets under management.

**Actively Managed Fund** — A fund where a manager makes ongoing decisions attempting to outperform a benchmark.

**Passively Managed Fund** — A fund designed simply to replicate the performance of an index, generally at lower cost.

**Target-Date Fund** — A fund that automatically adjusts its asset mix to become more conservative as a target date (often retirement) approaches.

---

## 6. Risk, Volatility, and Portfolio Concepts

**Volatility** — The degree and speed at which a security's price moves up and down over time.

**Diversification** — Spreading investments across different assets, sectors, or geographies to reduce the impact of any single investment's poor performance.

**Asset Allocation** — How an investor divides a portfolio among different asset classes (stocks, bonds, cash, etc.) based on goals and risk tolerance.

**Risk Tolerance** — An investor's capacity and willingness to endure losses in pursuit of returns.

**Beta** — A measure of a stock's volatility relative to the overall market; a beta above 1 indicates greater volatility than the market, below 1 indicates less.

**Correlation** — A measure of how closely two investments move in relation to one another.

**Liquidity** — How easily an asset can be bought or sold without significantly affecting its price.

**Drawdown** — The percentage decline from a portfolio's or asset's peak value to its subsequent low point.

**Capital Gain** — The profit realized when an asset is sold for more than its purchase price.

**Capital Loss** — The loss realized when an asset is sold for less than its purchase price.

**Unrealized Gain/Loss** — A paper profit or loss on an investment that hasn't yet been sold.

**Rebalancing** — Periodically adjusting a portfolio back to its intended asset allocation as investments grow or shrink at different rates.

**Dollar-Cost Averaging** — Investing a fixed amount of money at regular intervals regardless of price, which smooths out the average purchase price over time.

---

## 7. Trading Strategies and Behavior

**Long Position** — Owning a security with the expectation that its price will rise.

**Short Selling** — Borrowing and selling a security with the intention of buying it back later at a lower price, profiting from a decline; carries theoretically unlimited risk since a price can rise indefinitely.

**Margin** — Money borrowed from a broker to purchase securities, using existing holdings as collateral.

**Margin Call** — A broker's demand for additional funds or securities when an account's value falls below a required maintenance level.

**Day Trading** — Buying and selling securities within the same trading day, closing all positions before the market closes.

**Swing Trading** — Holding positions for several days to weeks to capture short- to medium-term price moves.

**Buy and Hold** — A long-term strategy of purchasing securities and holding them regardless of short-term market fluctuations.

**Technical Analysis** — Evaluating securities by studying historical price and volume patterns on charts.

**Fundamental Analysis** — Evaluating a security by examining a company's financial health, earnings, and broader economic factors.

**Momentum** — The tendency of a security's recent price trend to continue in the same direction over the near term.

---

## 8. Corporate Actions and Company Events

**Stock Split** — When a company increases its number of outstanding shares by issuing more shares to current holders, lowering the price per share proportionally without changing total value.

**Reverse Stock Split** — The opposite of a stock split — a company reduces its number of outstanding shares, raising the price per share proportionally.

**Buyback (Share Repurchase)** — When a company repurchases its own shares from the market, typically reducing the number of outstanding shares and often supporting the stock price.

**Earnings Report** — A company's quarterly or annual disclosure of its financial performance, closely watched by investors.

**Earnings Season** — The period several times a year when most public companies release their quarterly earnings reports.

**Guidance** — A company's own forecast of its expected future financial performance.

**Merger** — The combination of two companies into a single new entity.

**Acquisition** — When one company purchases and takes control of another.

**Spinoff** — When a company separates part of its business into a new, independently traded company.

**Delisting** — The removal of a company's stock from an exchange, often due to failure to meet listing requirements or a merger/acquisition/bankruptcy.

---

## 9. Accounts, Regulation, and Mechanics

**Brokerage Account** — An account held with a licensed firm that allows an investor to buy and sell securities.

**Broker** — A licensed individual or firm that executes buy and sell orders on behalf of investors.

**Robo-Advisor** — An automated platform that builds and manages a portfolio based on an investor's goals and risk profile, typically at lower cost than a human advisor.

**SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission)** — The U.S. federal agency responsible for regulating securities markets and protecting investors.

**FINRA (Financial Industry Regulatory Authority)** — A self-regulatory organization overseeing U.S. brokerage firms and exchange markets.

**SIPC (Securities Investor Protection Corporation)** — An organization that protects investors' brokerage accounts (not against investment losses, but against brokerage firm failure) up to certain limits.

**Settlement Date** — The date by which a securities trade must be finalized, with cash and shares officially exchanged (typically one business day after the trade in the U.S., known as T+1).

**Ex-Dividend Date** — The date on or after which a buyer of a stock is no longer entitled to the most recently declared dividend.

**Record Date** — The date a company uses to determine which shareholders are entitled to receive a declared dividend.

**Prospectus** — A formal legal document disclosing details about a security offering, required for IPOs and mutual funds.

**10-K** — A comprehensive annual report a public company is required to file with the SEC, detailing financial performance and risks.

**10-Q** — A public company's required quarterly financial report filed with the SEC, less detailed than the 10-K.

## 10. A Few Extra Terms Worth Knowing (Often Left Off Beginner Lists)

**Circuit Breaker** — An exchange mechanism that automatically halts trading market-wide for a set period after a severe, rapid price decline, intended to curb panic selling.

**After-Hours Trading** — Trading that occurs outside standard exchange hours, typically with lower liquidity and wider spreads than regular sessions.

**Pre-Market Trading** — Trading that takes place before the standard market opening bell, often driven by overnight news or earnings releases.

**52-Week High/Low** — The highest and lowest prices at which a stock has traded over the past year, commonly used as a quick reference point for a stock's recent range.

**Yield Curve** — A line plotting interest rates of bonds with different maturities, closely watched as a broader economic indicator that can influence stock markets.

**Inflation** — A general rise in prices across an economy over time, which erodes purchasing power and can influence central bank policy and stock valuations.

**Federal Reserve (The Fed)** — The U.S. central bank, whose interest rate decisions significantly influence borrowing costs, corporate earnings expectations, and stock market sentiment.

**Sector Rotation** — The practice of shifting investment among different sectors based on where the economic cycle suggests the strongest performance is likely.

**Institutional Investor** — A large organization (pension fund, insurance company, mutual fund) that trades securities in large volumes, generally subject to less regulatory protection than individual retail investors.

**Retail Investor** — An individual, non-professional investor buying and selling securities for their own personal account.

**Insider Trading** — Buying or selling a security based on material, non-public information about the company — illegal in most regulated markets.

**Wash Sale** — A tax rule that disallows claiming a loss on a security sold at a loss if a substantially identical security is purchased within 30 days before or after the sale.

**Tax-Loss Harvesting** — Deliberately selling losing investments to realize a capital loss that can offset taxable gains elsewhere in a portfolio.

**Order Book** — The real-time list of buy and sell orders for a security at various prices, showing depth of market interest.

**Slippage** — The difference between the expected price of a trade and the price at which it actually executes, often occurring in fast-moving or thin markets.

**Fractional Share** — Ownership of less than one full share of a stock, allowing investors to buy a specific dollar amount rather than a whole share.

**Payment for Order Flow (PFOF)** — A practice where brokers receive compensation from market makers for routing customer orders to them, part of how many "commission-free" brokers generate revenue.

**Watchlist** — A list of securities an investor tracks for potential future buying or selling, without necessarily holding a position yet.

---

*This glossary was written from general financial-market knowledge as an original reference document. Definitions are simplified for beginners; always verify specifics (e.g., current settlement timelines or regulatory limits) before making investment decisions, as rules and market conventions can change.*

## 11. Terms From the Stocks & ETFs Track

### Chapter 1 — What You Actually Own

**Limited liability** — The rule that a shareholder's loss is capped at what they paid for the shares. Creditors of the company have no claim on the shareholder's other assets. *(Lesson 1)*

**Residual claim** — A claim on what remains after all other obligations are met. Shareholders hold one; lenders do not. *(Lesson 1)*

**Share** — A unit of ownership in a company. Owning one makes you a part-owner of the business itself — not a lender to it, and not a customer of it. *(Lesson 1)*

**Share class** — A category of share within the same company. Different classes can carry different voting rights while having an identical claim on the profits. *(Lesson 1)*

**Expected value** — The average result of an activity if it were repeated many times: each possible outcome multiplied by its probability, all added together. *(Lesson 2)*

**Positive-sum** — An activity where the total value held by all participants can grow, because value is being created outside the transactions themselves. *(Lesson 2)*

**Zero-sum** — An activity where one participant's gain is exactly another's loss. Nothing is created; the pot is only redistributed. *(Lesson 2)*

**Ask** — The lowest price anyone is currently willing to sell it for. Also called the offer. *(Lesson 3)*

**Bid** — The highest price anyone is currently willing to pay for the share. *(Lesson 3)*

**Settlement** — The process of actually exchanging shares for money after a trade is agreed. It happens a set number of business days after the trade date. *(Lesson 3)*

**Divisor** — A number an index is divided by so splits and membership changes do not create artificial jumps. *(Lesson 4)*

**Equal weighting** — Giving every company in the index the same weight regardless of its size or share price, and restoring those weights periodically. *(Lesson 4)*

**Index** — A rule for combining the prices of a chosen set of shares into one number, so the group can be tracked over time. *(Lesson 4)*

**Market-capitalisation weighting** — Weighting each company by its total market value, so a company worth twice as much has twice the influence on the index. *(Lesson 4)*

**Price weighting** — Weighting each company by its share price alone, regardless of the size of the company. *(Lesson 4)*

**Reconstitution** — The periodic review in which an index provider adds and removes companies according to its published rules. *(Lesson 4)*

**Accumulating and distributing shares** — An accumulating fund reinvests dividends inside the fund. A distributing fund pays them out to you as cash. *(Lesson 5)*

**Authorised participant** — A large financial firm permitted to create new ETF shares or redeem existing ones by trading directly with the fund. *(Lesson 5)*

**Premium and discount** — An ETF trading above its NAV is at a premium; below it, a discount. *(Lesson 5)*

**Securities lending** — A fund lending the shares it holds to other market participants in exchange for a fee, while holding collateral against the loan. *(Lesson 5)*

**Tracking difference** — The gap between a fund's return and its index's return over a period. Tracking error is the variability of that gap. *(Lesson 5)*

**Payout ratio** — The proportion of a company's earnings paid out as dividends. A ratio above 100% means the company is paying out more than it earned. *(Lesson 6)*

**Share buyback** — A company using cash to buy its own shares in the market and cancel them, leaving each remaining share entitled to a larger slice of the same business. *(Lesson 6)*

**Total return** — The return from price change and dividends together, with dividends assumed reinvested. Price return counts only the price change. *(Lesson 6)*

**Active management** — Selecting holdings in an attempt to beat an index, rather than tracking it. *(Lesson 7)*

**Ongoing charges figure** — A standardised annual cost figure for a fund, covering management and most operating costs. Also encountered as the total expense ratio, and broadly comparable to the American expense ratio. *(Lesson 7)*

**Passive management** — Holding the market in proportion, tracking an index rather than trying to beat it. *(Lesson 7)*


### Chapter 2 — Reading a Company and a Market

**Balance sheet** — What the company owns and owes at a single moment. It answers: what does the business consist of right now? *(Lesson 8)*

**Cash flow statement** — Where cash actually came from and went over a period. It answers: did money genuinely move? *(Lesson 8)*

**Free cash flow** — Cash generated by operations, less the spending needed to maintain and expand the company's assets. Roughly, the cash genuinely available to the owners. *(Lesson 8)*

**Income statement** — What the company earned and spent over a period. It answers: was the business profitable during these months? *(Lesson 8)*

**Receivables** — Money owed to the company by customers who have been billed but have not yet paid. *(Lesson 8)*

**Share count** — The number of shares the company has issued. Earnings per share, book value per share and dividends per share are all divided by it. *(Lesson 8)*

**Cyclical company** — A company whose earnings rise and fall sharply with the economic cycle — miners, carmakers, chemicals, shipping and similar. *(Lesson 9)*

**Enterprise value to EBITDA** — Company value including debt, divided by earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. Used to compare businesses with different levels of borrowing. *(Lesson 9)*

**Trailing and forward P/E** — A trailing P/E uses earnings already reported. A forward P/E uses analysts' estimates of earnings not yet earned. *(Lesson 9)*

**Book-to-market** — Accounting equity divided by market value. High means the market price is low relative to the books. *(Lesson 10)*

**Factor tilt** — Holding a broad, diversified fund constructed to lean toward a characteristic such as value, size or momentum, rather than selecting individual companies. *(Lesson 10)*

**Growth investing** — Buying shares that look expensive against those measures, on the expectation that the underlying business will grow into and past the price. *(Lesson 10)*

**Implementation cost** — The trading costs, spreads, taxes and fees incurred in actually holding a strategy, which are absent from the paper describing it. *(Lesson 10)*

**Value investing** — Buying shares that look cheap against a fundamental measure such as book value or earnings, on the expectation that the market has been too pessimistic. *(Lesson 10)*

**Value premium** — The historically higher average return of high book-to-market shares. *(Lesson 10)*

**Market efficiency** — The idea that share prices already reflect available information, so that information cannot be used to earn better-than-average returns. *(Lesson 11)*

**Semi-strong form** — All public information is already reflected. *(Lesson 11)*

**Weak form** — Past prices are already reflected in the current price. *(Lesson 11)*

**Home bias** — The tendency to hold a much larger share of domestic companies than their weight in the world market would suggest. *(Lesson 12)*

**Market risk** — Risk that affects nearly all companies together — a recession, a rate shock, a war. No amount of diversification within equities removes it. *(Lesson 12)*

**Specific risk** — Risk attached to one company — a failed product, a fire, a fraud. It can be diversified away, because these events are largely unrelated across companies. *(Lesson 12)*


### Chapter 3 — Risk and Position Sizing for Equities

**Inflation risk** — The loss of purchasing power over time, which affects money held in cash more severely than money held in productive assets. *(Lesson 13)*

**Permanent capital loss** — Value that is not coming back — a company that fails, or a holding sold at the bottom and never rebought. *(Lesson 13)*

**Sequence-of-returns risk** — The risk that the order in which returns arrive damages you, even when the average return is unchanged. *(Lesson 13)*

**Standard deviation** — A statistical measure of how widely returns have varied around their average. The usual industry shorthand for risk. *(Lesson 13)*

**Averaging down** — Buying more of a holding after its price has fallen, reducing the average price paid. *(Lesson 14)*

**Concentration** — Holding a large share of a portfolio in one company, industry or country. *(Lesson 14)*

**Position drift** — The growth of a successful holding into a larger share of the portfolio than was ever chosen, simply because it rose. *(Lesson 14)*

**Position size** — How much of your total portfolio is held in one investment, usually as a percentage. *(Lesson 14)*

**Lump-sum investing** — Investing the whole amount at once. *(Lesson 15)*

**Borrow fee** — The annual rate charged for borrowing shares to short. It rises with demand and can become extreme on a company many people want to short. *(Lesson 16)*

**Daily leveraged fund** — A fund that aims to deliver a multiple of an index's return over a single day, resetting its exposure at the end of each day. *(Lesson 16)*

**Maintenance margin** — The minimum proportion of a margined position that must be covered by your own money. Fall below it and the broker demands more or sells. *(Lesson 16)*


### Chapter 4 — Behaviour, Costs and a Written Plan

**Behaviour gap** — The difference between a fund's published return and the return actually earned by the average unit of money invested in it, caused by money arriving and leaving at different times. *(Lesson 17)*

**Dollar-weighted return** — The return earned by the actual money invested, weighted by how much was present at each moment. Also called the money-weighted or internal rate of return. *(Lesson 17)*

**Performance chasing** — Moving money into a fund after a strong run and out after a weak one, so purchases cluster near highs and sales near lows. *(Lesson 17)*

**Time-weighted return** — The return of the fund itself, ignoring when money went in or out. This is the number in the fund's marketing material. *(Lesson 17)*

**Fee drag** — The cumulative effect of annual charges on a compounding balance. It grows with both the rate charged and the length of time held. *(Lesson 18)*

**Fee stack** — The full set of recurring charges on a holding: the fund charge, the platform or custody charge, and any advice charge, added together. *(Lesson 18)*

**Tax drag** — The reduction in compounding caused by paying tax on gains, dividends or interest along the way rather than at the end. *(Lesson 18)*

**Investment policy statement** — A short written document setting out what you will hold, in what proportions, how often you will change it, and under what conditions you will sell. Written before investing, consulted during stress. *(Lesson 19)*

**Rebalancing band** — A rule that returns a holding to its target weight only once it drifts beyond a set distance from it — for example, a target of 30% with a band of 5 points, acted on at 25% or 35%. *(Lesson 19)*


### Chapter 5 — Real-World Case Studies

**Narrative valuation** — Pricing an asset on a story about its future rather than on observable cash flows. Not always wrong, but unfalsifiable while the story lasts. *(Lesson 20)*

**Drawdown duration** — The time from a peak to the recovery of that peak, as distinct from the depth of the fall in between. *(Lesson 21)*

**Clearing margin** — Collateral a broker must post to the central clearing house to cover the risk of trades between execution and settlement. *(Lesson 22)*

**Gamma squeeze** — A rise driven by dealers who sold call options buying the underlying shares to hedge their exposure, buying more as the price rises. *(Lesson 22)*

**Short squeeze** — A rise driven by short sellers being forced to buy back shares to close their positions, whose buying pushes the price higher, forcing more shorts to buy. *(Lesson 22)*


### Chapter 6 — A Professional Equity Process

**Shareholder yield** — What a holding returns to owners through dividends and buybacks combined — the equity form of carry. *(Lesson 23)*

**Trade thesis** — A written, structured argument for why a specific position should work, built from several independent lines of evidence rather than one opinion. *(Lesson 23)*

**Market regime** — The prevailing environment: whether risk appetite is rising or falling, whether volatility is low or high, and whether prices are trending or ranging. *(Lesson 24)*

**No-trade regime** — A market with no clear trend, no imminent catalyst and no pillar strongly aligned. The correct position is often none. *(Lesson 24)*

**Transmission chain** — The path by which a change in monetary policy moves across markets: interest rates first, then currencies and bonds, then credit and equities, then commodities and other risk assets. *(Lesson 24)*

**Correlation clustering** — The tendency of separate markets to move together far more closely during sharp falls than during normal or rising periods. *(Lesson 25)*

**Process review** — Examining a closed decision for whether the reasoning and the rules were sound, rather than only for whether it made money. *(Lesson 25)*
