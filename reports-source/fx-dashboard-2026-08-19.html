Educational Breakdown — Long Brent Crude (Invalidated, –2.03%)

What we expected, and why: We anticipated Brent crude would rally from the $89.50–91.50 entry zone to the $102–108 target, driven by Strait of Hormuz supply disruptions and persistent Middle East geopolitical risk. The original thesis (73/100 confidence, published July 30) scored highest on Macro (33/35), citing shipping constraints through Hormuz, seasonal demand (summer driving in Northern Hemisphere), and OPEC+ supply discipline as the core catalysts. Technicals (20/25) pointed to a constructive chart with support at $88 and resistance cleared at $92, suggesting room to run toward triple digits.

What actually happened: Brent fluctuated around $88.50 on August 17 as traders weighed geopolitical tensions against signs more oil was moving through the Strait of Hormuz than initially expected; Gulf producers appeared to be maintaining substantial shipments from the Persian Gulf despite the ongoing conflict, helping limit supply shock concerns. The pair failed to sustain above $92, retested the $87–88 floor multiple times, and ultimately stopped out at $87.75. Current price $90.97 (Aug 18) is back near the entry zone but the stop was hit intraday earlier in the period.

Match or divergence — and why: Divergence on supply resilience. The macro thesis correctly identified Hormuz tensions as a risk premium driver, but it overestimated the severity of the supply disruption. The key miss: Middle Eastern producers became increasingly adept at covertly moving oil through Hormuz to global buyers while also supplying cargoes from outside the key chokepoint; Gulf producers maintained substantial shipments despite the conflict. We modeled a scenario where Hormuz constraints would meaningfully tighten physical supply and drive spot prices higher; instead, the market saw a "headline risk premium" that faded as traders realized supply was flowing more freely than feared. The geopolitical risk was real, but the supply response (both from producers finding workarounds and from strategic stockpile draws) was faster and more effective than the thesis anticipated. Additionally, demand proved softer than the "summer driving season" narrative suggested — Chinese economic data remained mixed, and US gasoline demand was flat. The technical stop at $87.75 was set just below the entry zone to limit risk, and it correctly capped the loss; without it, the trade could have extended further into the red as oil tested the low $80s in early August.
